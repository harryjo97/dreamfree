What makes imagination-free WAMs scale?

DreamFree adds future representation distillation to video generative training, making the video model’s predictive knowledge available in the features used for action prediction. This helps translate larger video backbones into better control; generative training alone shows little benefit from scaling.

Make video priors useful without imagining videos.

An explicit alignment loss makes current-frame features predictive of the future.

At training

STAGE 1

Distill predictive representations

A frozen video diffusion teacher observes current and future frames. An alignment loss trains the current-frame features to predict the teacher’s future-aware representations, alongside the original video generative objective. This directly supervises the features used for control and needs no action labels.

STAGE 2

Learn to read out actions

Using action-labeled robot demonstrations, a readout module extracts action-relevant features from the visual encoder. An action decoder maps these features to executable action chunks.

At inference

Evaluation results

RoboCasa-Kitchen simulation

Across 24 kitchen tasks, DreamFree reaches 71.7% success, rising to 74.4% with extra video data. Scaling the backbone from 5B to 14B adds 13.2 percentage points, compared with 0.3 for Fast-WAM.

Real-time control at 11 Hz

With its 14B video backbone, DreamFree averages 91 ms per action chunk on a single H200. Inference is 17.8× faster than DreamZero, without video step distillation or asynchronous execution.

Task transfer from video demonstrations

DreamFree learns from video-only demonstrations through its predictive encoder.

Atomic tasks use one skill; composite tasks combine several skills in sequence.

Composite videos in pre-training video-only fine-tuning

We add composite-task demonstrations to visual encoder pre-training, alongside atomic-task videos. Both models use the same atomic-task action labels to train the action module; no composite-task action labels are used.

2× playback

Open the microwave → put food inside → close the door → turn it on.

Note: The atomic training data typically includes the individual skills used in composite tasks, e.g., “Open the microwave” and “Close the door.”

Our results suggest that these demonstrations help it combine familiar skills into new task sequences, without additional action labels.

The policy completes some tasks with limited training data, but folding clothes and erasing the whiteboard are still unreliable. The t-shirt folding failure may reflect difficulty understanding the cloth’s state.
